# Founder Amendment 02 — Layered Precommitment and All-Surface Protection

Status: Founder vision captured; Cycle 2 design amendment; not implementation authorization  
Date: 2026-07-15  
Applies to: Threads 01, 04, 05, 07, and 10

## 1. Founder intent

Better Life should not be reduced to a Chrome blocker or a single app. The long-term vision is a user-authored protection system that can coordinate many visible layers across browsers, operating systems, devices, networks, and existing platform controls.

The founder's desired outcome is not mathematical impossibility of bypass. It is to make impulsive reversal sufficiently slow, effortful, visible, and support-connected that the user's calm-state decision has a realistic chance to survive a high-risk moment.

This amendment preserves the following ambitions:

- all relevant browsers and browsing surfaces, not Chrome alone;
- phones, tablets, desktops, TVs, consoles, routers, and other practical access paths;
- guided activation of official controls that already exist but are difficult for users to discover or configure;
- multiple independent protection layers rather than one fragile blocker;
- user-authored cooling-off periods before weakening protection;
- staged reversal instead of one-tap collapse;
- visible support escalation when the user requests help;
- recovery from partial layer failure;
- honest documentation of bypass, reset, replacement-device, alternate-network, and account-level limitations.

## 2. Safety correction: strong must not mean hidden

Better Life may be persistent, layered, inconvenient to weaken, and difficult to bypass. It must not become covert, deceptive, or externally controlled.

The following are rejected design patterns:

- hidden scripts, hidden services, secret files, or undisclosed background software;
- dormant or undocumented functionality;
- installing unrelated software without clear user action and disclosure;
- storing enforcement code in secret locations to survive uninstall;
- leaving restrictions behind after uninstall without a visible ownership and cleanup model;
- secret third-party administration;
- a spouse, partner, employer, religious group, school, or organization holding unilateral permanent control;
- intentionally confusing the user, using forgotten credentials as a safety mechanism, or designing an endless maze with no understandable exit;
- claiming that the system is impossible to bypass.

Strong protection must come from explicit enrollment, visible active layers, user-authored policy, cooling-off, staged deactivation, transparent recovery, and bounded trusted-support roles.

## 3. Product modes

### 3.1 Supportive Mode

Purpose: help without restricting immediate choice.

Capabilities may include:

- manual Protective Spiral;
- calm-state plan;
- values reminder;
- education;
- coping action;
- optional AI conversation after separate approval;
- optional support request;
- immediate exit.

This is the lowest-power mode and the safest place to test language, usefulness, agency, burden, and adverse effects.

### 3.2 Friction Mode

Purpose: create a deliberate pause between impulse and action.

Capabilities may include:

- block or redirect on selected domains or apps where supported;
- user-selected delay;
- one short Protective Spiral action;
- repeated confirmation;
- alternate healthy action;
- visible legitimate-use recovery;
- transparent explanation of the active layer and its limitations.

Friction is not punishment. It must not use shame, fear, streak loss, moral scoring, or artificial confusion.

### 3.3 Precommitted Strict Mode

Purpose: protect a calm-state decision from immediate high-risk reversal.

Possible controls, subject to platform feasibility and safety review:

- fixed protection term;
- scheduled windows;
- cooling-off before weakening policy;
- staged deactivation workflow;
- re-authentication;
- delayed configuration change;
- explicit review of what will be disabled;
- optional trusted-support acknowledgement;
- optional multi-device policy where technically feasible;
- visible emergency and legitimate-use recovery paths;
- expiry and renewal rather than indefinite lock-in.

Strict Mode is voluntary self-binding, not third-party ownership.

## 4. All-browser and all-surface strategy

The target is not one universal browser extension. The target is a shared protection policy with multiple platform adapters.

### 4.1 Browser adapters

Thread 01 must evaluate at least:

- Chrome;
- Microsoft Edge;
- Firefox;
- Safari;
- Chromium-derived browsers such as Brave, Opera, and Vivaldi where extension compatibility exists;
- browser profiles, private/incognito windows, guest profiles, portable browsers, and extension disable/removal;
- in-app browsers and embedded web views;
- mobile browser limitations;
- TV browsers and unsupported browsers;
- remote browsing, virtual machines, and alternate user accounts.

Each browser must be classified separately as:

- directly supported;
- compatible through a shared extension base;
- supported through a browser-specific adapter;
- partially covered by OS/network controls;
- unsupported;
- research required.

No artifact may say “all browsers are protected” without a tested capability matrix.

### 4.2 Shared policy, separate enforcement

A future architecture may use:

- one user-authored policy model;
- browser-specific adapters;
- OS-specific adapters;
- network/DNS/VPN adapters;
- native platform-control setup guidance;
- status and health checks;
- explicit degraded-state reporting.

The policy may be common; enforcement is platform-specific.

## 5. Native Control Orchestrator

Better Life should research a `Native Control Orchestrator`: a guided setup system that discovers relevant official controls and helps the user activate them with the least possible effort.

Candidate control families include:

- Apple Screen Time and Family Controls capabilities where available and approved;
- Android Digital Wellbeing, app limits, notification controls, DNS, VPN, and device-policy capabilities according to consumer versus managed-device status;
- Windows and macOS account, browser, application, DNS, firewall, and family/digital-wellbeing controls;
- browser SafeSearch and restricted-content settings;
- router and DNS filtering;
- smart-TV and console restrictions;
- app-native safety, restricted mode, mute, block, search filtering, and content-preference settings.

The orchestrator must distinguish:

1. can be configured programmatically by an ordinary consumer app;
2. requires a system permission or entitlement;
3. requires the user to complete a guided settings flow;
4. requires managed-device enrollment;
5. requires router/admin credentials;
6. cannot be configured by Better Life and can only be explained.

It must never claim automatic setup where the operating system requires explicit user action.

## 6. Layered Protection Stack

A future Strict Mode may combine independent, visible layers.

Example stack:

1. Personal policy and protection schedule.
2. Browser adapter rules.
3. OS-native app/site restrictions.
4. Local DNS or VPN filtering.
5. Router/DNS filtering for the home network.
6. Search and content-safety settings.
7. App-native restrictions.
8. Protective Spiral redirect.
9. Cooling-off for weakening the policy.
10. Optional trusted-support escalation.

Every layer must have:

- owner;
- activation method;
- permission;
- scope;
- data processed;
- visible status;
- bypass modes;
- false-positive recovery;
- disable path;
- uninstall behavior;
- cleanup behavior;
- failure notification;
- test evidence.

## 7. Reversal Ladder, not hidden maze

The founder wants a long sequence that buys time. Better Life should implement this as an understandable `Reversal Ladder`, not an intentionally confusing maze.

Possible steps:

1. User requests to weaken or disable protection.
2. System shows the calm-state policy and reason.
3. User completes re-authentication.
4. User selects what layer they want to change.
5. System shows affected devices and protections.
6. Cooling-off begins.
7. User may cancel the disable request during cooling-off.
8. Optional trusted support is notified only if the user explicitly configured that exact event.
9. At expiry, the user confirms again.
10. Layers are disabled in a controlled order.
11. Cleanup is verified and reported.

The purpose is time, reflection, and informed reversal—not confusion, memory failure, or helplessness.

## 8. Credential and trusted-support model

### 8.1 User credentials

Better Life must not depend on the user forgetting a password. Forgotten credentials are a reliability and lockout risk, not a product mechanism.

Possible safe mechanisms include:

- device re-authentication;
- passkey;
- recovery code;
- time-delayed recovery;
- user-controlled key escrow with clear risks;
- multi-party recovery only after expert security and coercion review.

### 8.2 Trusted-support role

A trusted person may have a bounded role only when the user explicitly chooses it.

Possible bounded roles:

- receive a predefined support request;
- acknowledge a deactivation request;
- provide one approval factor during a limited term;
- participate in a two-of-three recovery model;
- receive a tamper or reset-risk alert.

Prohibited roles:

- secret administrator;
- permanent owner of the user's account or device;
- unilateral indefinite veto;
- access to browsing history, explicit content, private notes, location, or hidden risk scores;
- changing restrictions without the user's visible consent.

Strict Mode must include coercion screening, revocation rules, relationship-change handling, and a safe escape process.

## 9. Uninstall and cleanup semantics

The system must explicitly choose one of these models per layer:

### Model A — App-owned layer

Uninstall removes the app and its app-owned restrictions. This is simple and transparent but easy to bypass.

### Model B — User-owned external layer

The app helps the user configure a visible external control such as a router, DNS service, operating-system setting, or browser policy. Uninstalling Better Life does not automatically remove that external control because the user owns it independently.

Requirements:

- the user is told before activation;
- Better Life shows an inventory of external layers;
- cleanup instructions exist;
- ownership and credentials are clear;
- there is no secret persistence.

### Model C — Managed-device layer

Only where legally, technically, and ethically appropriate, a user may enroll a personally owned device into a managed configuration.

Requirements:

- explicit enrollment;
- clear consumer-versus-enterprise distinction;
- visible management status;
- tested unenrollment and recovery;
- no deceptive use of enterprise power;
- specialist review before any product commitment.

## 10. Factory reset, replacement device, and alternate path

Factory reset remains a fundamental bypass or failure path on many devices. Better Life must not promise otherwise.

Research should evaluate:

- whether a pre-reset event can be detected at all;
- what happens when the app is uninstalled first;
- offline reset;
- recovery mode;
- new device setup;
- alternate Apple/Google/Microsoft account;
- borrowed device;
- old phone;
- work device;
- TV, console, or browser not enrolled;
- cellular data, hotspot, alternate Wi-Fi, VPN, encrypted DNS, and remote desktop.

An alert to a trusted person or support network may be offered only when:

- the user explicitly selected the exact event category;
- the event is technically detectable;
- delivery is not presented as guaranteed;
- the message contains minimum information;
- it does not reveal intimate behavior;
- the user can review and revoke the rule in a calm state.

No documentation may imply that an offline factory reset can always be detected or prevented.

## 11. Anonymous network escalation

The founder's vision includes sending a help signal to an anonymous support network when protection is under stress.

This remains a future, separately gated capability.

Before any live network test, Thread 04 must establish:

- bounded pseudonymity rather than absolute anonymity;
- adult eligibility;
- moderation operations;
- abuse prevention;
- no direct exchange of identity, contact, location, or handles;
- minimum-disclosure support signals;
- no automatic broadcast of intimate details;
- user confirmation rules;
- incident response;
- legal/privacy review;
- funded human safety operations.

A trusted chosen ally should be evaluated before stranger matching.

## 12. Technical truth standard

The target statement is:

> Better Life may make bypass meaningfully slower, more effortful, more visible, and more support-connected across multiple user-approved layers.

The prohibited statement is:

> Better Life cannot be bypassed in practice.

Any practical-resistance claim requires:

- platform-specific testing;
- threat model;
- bypass matrix;
- real-device testing;
- red-team testing;
- user testing;
- false-positive and lockout testing;
- explicit residual risk.
