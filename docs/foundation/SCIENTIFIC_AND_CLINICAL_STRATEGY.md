# Better Life — Scientific And Clinical Strategy

Status: v0.1  
Date: 2026-07-10

## Purpose

Better Life will be evidence-informed, clinically honest, safety-reviewed, and continuously updated. The company will not select one theory of addiction, one therapeutic school, or one moral framework as unquestioned truth.

The first product is behavioral technology, not clinical treatment. However, because it touches compulsive sexual behavior, pornography use, distress, shame, relationships, mental health, and safety, clinical and scientific governance must be built from the beginning.

## 1. Scientific Framing

The initial evidence program must distinguish:

- high sexual desire without impairment;
- unwanted habits;
- problematic pornography use;
- compulsive sexual behavior disorder (CSBD);
- distress driven primarily by moral or religious incongruence;
- relationship conflict;
- co-occurring depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, trauma, ADHD, bipolar-spectrum symptoms, substance use, loneliness, sleep disruption, or other conditions;
- acute safety risk.

Better Life must not label all frequent sexual behavior or pornography use as addiction.

## 2. Evidence Domains

The research program will maintain evidence maps across:

### Clinical classification and assessment

- ICD-11 CSBD criteria and differential considerations;
- validated assessment instruments;
- functional impairment;
- moral incongruence;
- comorbidity;
- severity and treatment-seeking behavior.

### Psychotherapy and behavioral support

Evaluate evidence, mechanisms, applicability, risks, and product-safe translations for:

- Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT);
- Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT);
- mindfulness-based approaches;
- motivational interviewing;
- relapse-prevention models;
- stimulus control and environmental restructuring;
- implementation intentions;
- urge surfing and distress tolerance;
- emotion-regulation skills;
- self-compassion and shame reduction;
- values clarification;
- behavioral activation;
- habit reversal and competing responses where relevant;
- couples or relationship interventions where appropriate;
- peer support and structured groups;
- digital therapeutics and just-in-time adaptive interventions.

No technique will be presented as proven for this product merely because it is used in another condition.

### Neuroscience and learning

Evaluate without oversimplifying:

- cue reactivity;
- reward learning;
- reinforcement;
- habit formation;
- attentional bias;
- inhibitory control;
- stress and emotion regulation;
- sleep and executive function;
- state-dependent decision making;
- craving and temporal discounting;
- neuroplasticity.

Avoid deterministic language such as “your brain is permanently rewired” unless supported for the precise claim. The product should communicate that learned patterns can be strong and changeable.

### Human-computer interaction and behavior design

- precommitment and commitment devices;
- friction and delay;
- defaults;
- choice architecture;
- digital self-control tools;
- persuasive technology ethics;
- just-in-time interventions;
- accessibility;
- calm technology;
- trust and privacy comprehension;
- unintended reactance and workarounds.

### Privacy, safety, ethics, and law

- GDPR and special-category data;
- German and EU digital-health and consumer rules;
- medical-device boundary analysis;
- AI Act applicability;
- app-store policies;
- informed consent;
- coercive control and intimate-partner abuse risks;
- research ethics;
- data minimization;
- cybersecurity;
- age assurance and child-safety boundaries for any future expansion.

## 3. Evidence Hierarchy

Use this order when possible:

1. Official diagnostic and regulatory sources.
2. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines.
3. Randomized and controlled studies.
4. Prospective and longitudinal studies.
5. Validated observational research.
6. Qualitative research and lived experience.
7. Expert consensus and clinical practice knowledge.
8. Product analytics and experiments.
9. Anecdotes and founder hypotheses.

Lower-level evidence may generate ideas but must not be described as proof.

## 4. Clinical Advisory Network

Build a multidisciplinary advisory group rather than relying on one viewpoint.

Target expertise:

- clinical psychology;
- psychiatry;
- sex therapy and sexual medicine;
- behavioral addictions;
- CBT and ACT;
- trauma-informed practice;
- relationship and couples therapy;
- digital mental health;
- suicide and crisis safety;
- research ethics;
- privacy and data protection;
- coercive control / domestic abuse;
- lived-experience representation.

Advisors should disclose relevant commercial, ideological, religious, clinical, and advocacy interests.

## 5. NGOs, Associations, And Community Engagement

Map and engage organizations in several categories:

- sexual-health associations;
- mental-health charities;
- behavioral-addiction and digital-wellbeing organizations;
- evidence-based recovery groups;
- peer-support communities;
- digital-rights and privacy organizations;
- domestic-abuse and coercive-control organizations;
- public-health institutions;
- university research groups;
- professional psychotherapy and sex-therapy associations.

The goal is not endorsement hunting. It is to identify needs, harms, language, referral pathways, underserved groups, and governance expectations.

Do not partner with organizations that require hidden surveillance, public shaming, conversion practices, discrimination, or non-consensual control.

## 6. Research Workstreams

### Workstream A — Evidence map

Deliverables:

- living literature database;
- evidence summary by intervention;
- quality and bias notes;
- product implications;
- open questions;
- claims register.

### Workstream B — User discovery

Interview diverse adults with different:

- device ecosystems;
- severity levels;
- treatment histories;
- cultural and religious contexts;
- relationship contexts;
- genders and sexual orientations;
- privacy needs;
- prior blocker and accountability-tool experience.

Research must avoid collecting unnecessary explicit details.

### Workstream C — Expert discovery

Interview clinicians, researchers, coaches, group facilitators, privacy experts, and safety experts.

### Workstream D — Lived-experience co-design

Create a compensated, privacy-protected advisory group after the research protocol and compensation model are ready.

### Workstream E — Technical feasibility

Test platform capabilities and failure modes before making product promises.

### Workstream F — Safety and misuse

Use threat modeling and red-team scenarios for:

- partner spyware;
- coercive religious or family control;
- employer misuse;
- stalking;
- outing or data exposure;
- false crisis escalation;
- shame amplification;
- obsessive self-monitoring;
- discriminatory inference;
- harmful lock-in;
- bypass-related false confidence.

## 7. Initial Intervention Hypotheses

These are hypotheses, not approved product claims:

1. User-authored precommitment can reduce in-the-moment decision burden.
2. Layered environmental and device friction can create time for values-aligned choice.
3. Intervening earlier in a behavioral sequence may be more acceptable and useful than blocking only explicit content.
4. Short delay plus a meaningful replacement action may be more helpful than a warning alone.
5. Non-shaming lapse review may reduce concealment and improve learning.
6. Personalized high-risk windows may improve timing of support.
7. Minimal-disclosure human support may preserve dignity better than raw history or screenshot reporting.
8. Local-first intelligence may increase trust and reduce privacy risk.
9. Excessively strict or inaccurate controls may increase reactance, workarounds, abandonment, or harm.
10. Different user segments may need fundamentally different products rather than one universal mode.

## 8. Clinical Boundary And Referral

The product must clearly route beyond software when there is:

- imminent risk of self-harm or harm to others;
- severe functional impairment;
- suspected mania or psychosis;
- trauma requiring clinical care;
- abuse or coercive control;
- illegal or non-consensual behavior;
- risk involving minors;
- severe depression or inability to stay safe;
- need for diagnosis, medication, or psychotherapy.

Referral information must be localized, current, and reviewed.

## 9. Outcomes To Study

Potential outcomes include:

- frequency and duration of personally unwanted behavior;
- perceived control;
- functional impairment;
- distress, with moral incongruence analyzed separately where possible;
- self-efficacy;
- quality of life;
- work and sleep disruption;
- relationship impact;
- intervention usefulness;
- false positives and false negatives;
- bypass attempts;
- trust;
- privacy understanding;
- adverse events;
- shame;
- help-seeking;
- product dependence and successful tapering.

Outcome measures and study designs require expert review before clinical claims.

## 10. Initial Reference Anchors

The living evidence base should begin with and independently verify:

- World Health Organization ICD-11 entry for Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder (6C72).
- Kraus et al., “Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder in the ICD-11,” World Psychiatry (2018).
- Current systematic reviews of treatment approaches for problematic pornography use.
- Current meta-analyses of psychotherapy for problematic pornography use.
- Current sexual-medicine perspectives on assessment and treatment of CSBD.
- Current WFSBP guidance on assessment and pharmacological treatment of CSBD.
- Research on moral incongruence, treatment seeking, and cross-cultural assessment.
- Evidence and ethical literature on digital self-control tools, commitment devices, and just-in-time adaptive interventions.

Every citation added to product claims must be checked against the original paper, not only a summary, news article, or search result.

## 11. Decision Standard

A scientific idea enters the product only after we can state:

- what problem it addresses;
- for whom;
- by what mechanism;
- based on what evidence;
- with what uncertainty;
- with what risks;
- how it will be tested;
- what result would make us stop or redesign it.
